{¶1} Hactenus de officiis privatis: publica sunt vel politica vel ecclesiastica.

{¶2} Politica sunt magistratus et populi vel inter se vel erga vicinos et exteros.

{¶16} The opinion maintained by some, that obedience is due to the commands not only of an upright magistrate, but of an ususper, and that in matters contrary to justice, has no foundation in Scripture.[^s5] For with regard to 1 Pet. ii. 13. *submit yourselves to every ordinance of man,* it is evident from v. 14. that although this passage comprehends all human ordinances, all forms of government indiscriminately, it applies to them only so far as they are legitimately constituted. As for the obedience of the Israelites to Pharaoh, we have no means of

Besides, it is written, Psal. lx. 4. *thou hast given a banner to them that feared thee, that it may be displayed because of the truth.* That it may be the part of prudence to obey the commands even of a tyrant in lawful things, or, more properly, to comply with the necessity of the times for the sake of public peace, as well as of personal safety,[^s6] I am far from denying.

## headnote

**Place.** The last chapter of the *Doctrina*: having covered the private duties, Milton turns to the public ones, political and ecclesiastical. Both volumes close here — the Latin with *Totius operis finis*, the English with *The End*.

**Argument.** Political duties run three ways: magistrate towards people, people towards magistrate, and both towards neighbouring states. The magistrate is bound to justice, to moderate punishment, and to the sword used only against evil; the people to obedience, even under unjust rulers, but never in unlawful things. Between states Milton distinguishes lawful treaties of peace from unlawful alliances, and lays down seven rules of war. The ecclesiastical half treats ministers and church, each with its catalogue of opposites.

**Pressure point.** Twice Milton abandons proof-texts for sustained argument, and both times against coercion. Religion is to be defended by the magistrate, *not compelled*: a ruler who forces conscience errs as the pope errs, and courts the same name of antichrist; faith and conscience lie wholly outside civil judgement. And obedience to tyrants commanding wicked things rests on no divine authority — 1 Peter binds only to ordinances *legitimately constituted*, and what it says of servants gives no rule to free peoples.

that as a universal rule. In the Isaiah run of {¶7} — `Isa. i. 23. et iii. 5. et v. 13. et v. 15.

but **`et v. 23` is Isaiah chapter V, verse 23**: its words, *absolventibus improbum propter munus* /
*which justify the wicked for reward*, are Isa. 5:23 and nothing else. The same thing happens in
{¶29}, where `Jer. ii. 8. et v. 14.` is **Jeremiah 5**:14 (*eo quod loquimini verbum istud*), not
Jeremiah 2:14.

**And the English prints `v. 23` unchanged**, in the middle of a run where it corrected its three
neighbours. So the ambiguity is **not** resolved by laying the layers side by side; both volumes
carry it identically. ⚠ **For M4 this is the hardest citation problem the corpus has produced.**
`et v. N` cannot be resolved by form, by layer, or by the running chapter alone — **only the quoted
words identify the target**, and the index must therefore resolve these against the text of the verse,
not against the numeral. There are dozens of `et v. N` forms in this chapter.

**★★ Sumner RESOLVES Milton's verbal ranges into digits — six times — but not always.** The class
was first recorded in II.xvi (`Job. xxix. a v. 11. usque ad finem capitis` → `Job xxix. 11—25`).
This chapter has four distinct verbal forms and shows both behaviours:

| La | En | |
|---|---|---|
| et xxxi. a v. 1, ad v. 10 | xxxi. 1—10 | resolved |
| Num. xxxv. 6, &c. | Num. xxxv. 6—15 | resolved — an **open** `&c.` given an endpoint |
| et xxxiii. toto cap. a v. 2 | xxxiii. 2—31 | resolved |
| Amos. vii. 10. ad finem capitis | Amos vii. 10—17 | resolved |
| Mal. ii. 1, &c. ad v. 10 | Mal. ii. 1—10 | resolved |
| Ezech. xiii. toto cap. a v. 2 | Ezek. xiii. 2, &c. | **not** resolved |
| Lev. xxvi. per totum caput | See Levit. xxvi. | **dropped**, not resolved |

**For M4 this is a gift and a trap.** Where Sumner resolves, the English layer supplies a
machine-readable range Milton never wrote — usable, but it must be recorded as *the editor's*
resolution. Where he does not, the Latin's verbal form is the only witness. **Build the index from
the Latin, take the English's digits as an annotation, and never silently merge the two.**

**The dash-range rule (II.xv, confirmed II.xvi) holds again, and gains its cleanest proof.**
En 709 sets `xiv. 13—15, 18` for La's `xiv. 13, 14, 15, 18`: the three consecutive verses contract to
a dash, the discontinuous 18 stays an enumeration, in one citation. Runs of two are never contracted
anywhere in the chapter. **One exception is logged and it is not the rule failing:** La's
`Matt. xx. 25, 27, 28` (discontinuous) becomes En's `Matt. xx. 25—27`, which covers different verses —
a divergence, not a contraction.

**★★ The raised ordinals are back, seven of them in one paragraph, and the convention is STILL
unratified.** La {¶23} numbers Milton's rules of war `1ᵐ. 2ᵈᵒ. 3ᵗⁱᵒ. 4ᵗᵒ. 5ᵗᵒ. 6ᵗᵒ. 7ᵐᵒ.` — the
densest instance in the corpus, and the first where the series is the **structure of the paragraph**
rather than an incidental enumeration. Set inline as `1m.` `2do.` … following II.iv-b and II.xi, for
want of a superscript markup in CONVENTIONS §2. **The English renders them as plain words**
("first, Secondly, Thirdly, Fourthly, Fifthly, Sixthly, Seventhly"), which confirms on a seven-item
series what II.xi observed on one: **the feature exists in the Latin layer alone**, and no amount of
reading the English will recover it. ⚠ Three chapters now use an unratified notation; Book I will
hit it repeatedly.

**Other divergences and observations.**

- **★ Milton's `tyrannis` becomes "an ususper" (usurper) at En 703** — a lexical shift, not a
  misprint: a tyrant may hold office legitimately, a usurper by definition does not. The English
  narrows the claim at exactly the point where it is most exposed.
- **★ A politening on a live political claim.** La {¶29} says bishops formerly and *nunc magistratus
  **plerique*** — "now **most** magistrates" — appoint ministers over the people's suffrage. En:
  "is **not unfrequently** practised by magistrates in the present day." Same habit as II.vii, II.ix,
  II.x, II.xi, and running the same direction; note it is the opposite of II.xvi's addition, where
  Sumner *strengthened* Milton's sentence.
- En expands Milton's `eam præcipue ob causam pro Antichristo haberi` into "to be accounted
  antichrist, a name given to the pope himself chiefly from his encroachments on the consciences of
  mankind." An unpacking of what the Latin compresses rather than an addition — recorded as
  expansion, and deliberately distinguished from II.xvi's `nature never intended`, which supplies
  what the Latin does not contain.
- Narrowings: `Esth. iii. 2, 3, 4`→`iii. 2, 4` · `et lx. 1, 2, &c.`→`lx. 1` · `et cxliv. 1, 2`→
  `cxliv. 1` · `Isa. lvii. 2, &c.`→`lvi. 10` (with the correction). Widenings: `1 Pet. ii. 13`→
  `ii. 13, 14` (which is Milton's own argument at {¶16}) · `Dan. v. 18, 19`→`v. 18—20` ·
  `cap. xxv. 7, 8`→`xxv. 6—8`.
- Note [^s7] spans En 707–708 with the catchword **What**; as in II.xv, **the catchword tracks the
  footnote, not the running text.** Second instance of that in the corpus, both times on a note
