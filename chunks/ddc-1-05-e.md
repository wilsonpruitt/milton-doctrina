Non est meum,* mediatoris nimirum, ut vulgo interpretantur. At dubium non est, quin mater et filii tantam rem ambitiose petentes, totum Christum, quantuscunque esset, orarent, ut, quanto maxima vel hominis vel Dei potentia valebat, daret quod petebant. v. 20. *adorans et petens.* et 21. *dic ut sedeant.* His Christus etiam totus respondit, *non est meum;* et ne aliqua tamen ratione suum hoc esse putarent, non in sua quacunque sed in Patris potestate solius hoc esse testatur. ## headnote

**Place.** The fifth part of I.v. Part d ended with Milton's two-part programme. This part carries out the first half: where the Father and the Son are named together, the name, attributes, works and honour of God go to the Father alone.

**Argument.** A catena from the Gospels and Epistles gives the name *God* to the Father even where Christ is named first. The Son disclaims the divine attributes: omniscience (Matt. xxiv. 36, Mark xiii. 32), supreme dominion (*not mine to give*, Matt. xx. 23), supreme goodness (*none good but one*) and supreme glory. Milton refuses the usual escape, that Christ speaks as mediator or as man. After the hypostatic union Christ speaks as his whole person, and to split him by the sentence makes him a Proteus. Different will and understanding mean different essence. The works, including raising the Son from the dead, are the Father's, and one enigmatic John ii. 19 does not outweigh them. Worship and prayer go to the Father through the Son. Mal. iii. 1 means the Lord's coming in the flesh.

**Pressure point.** "Not mine to give" is the test case: if Christ answered only as mediator he would have used the logicians' *expositio prava*. The English follows Sumner's conjecture at the corrupt *par Patre* and drops Milton's Jude 25.

### Findings

**★★ `[^la8]`: the English silently follows Sumner's second conjecture.** The Latin prints *si ipsa par Patre, omnia tamen Patre accepta retulit*. Sumner calls the place *plane corruptus* and offers *patri* or *ipse par patri*. The English has *if he himself, being equal to the Father, gave back again*, which is *ipse par patri*, with no English note. Ledgered `uncertain`. This is the third time in I.v (after `[^la6]` and d's `[^la7]`) that the English adopts a Latin-volume conjecture without telling the reader.

**★★ The English drops Jude 25.** The Latin quotes Rom. xvi. 25–27 and then **Judæ 25.** *soli sapienti Deo servatori nostro gloria esto per Jesum Christum Dominum nostrum,* ut vetus interpres et nonnulli codices Græci habent. The English runs straight from Rom. xvi. 27 (*to God only wise, be glory through Jesus Christ*) into *—our Lord, as the Vetus Interpres and some of the Greek manuscripts read it.* The Jude reference and its words are gone, so the English attaches Milton's textual remark about the Vulgate and the Greek manuscripts to Romans. Both copies were read.

**★ En `{¶2 cont.}` *Salome*.** The Latin says only *mater et filii* (Matt. xx). Sumner names the mother (from Matt. xxvii. 56 and Mark xv. 40, by identification). The name is his addition.

**★ Note 4 cites the commentators.** Poole's *Synopsis* for the mediatorial reading of *not mine to give*, and Whitby against it. Note 2 gives the Greek of Col. ii. 2 and Macknight's rendering. Note 5 identifies the Horace (*Epist.* I. i. 90) and adds *PL* III. 603 (Proteus).

**`{¶2}` Horace:** *Quo teneam vultus mutantem Protea nodo?* is set as a display line in both volumes, and inline here (CONVENTIONS §2). The Latin prints it roman, not italic.
