{¶1} Hactenus de virtutibus sive officiis specialibus erga proximum, qua is proximus duntaxat est: nunc de iis quæ sunt **ERGA PROXIMUM NOBIS RATIONE ALIQUA RELATUM**. Eaque sunt vel privata vel publica.

{¶2} Privata sunt vel domestica vel externa. Gen. xviii. 19. *agnovi eum, ut quum præceperit filiis suis et familiæ suæ post se, etiam observent—.* 1 Tim. v. 8. *si quis suis, et maxime domesticis, non providet, fidem abnegavit, et est infideli deterior.*

{¶3} Officia domestica sunt fere mutua, mariti et uxoris, parentum et liberorum, fratrum et cognatorum inter se, heri et servorum.

{¶4} **MARITI ET UXORIS OFFICIA** sunt communia vel propria.

{¶5} Communia sunt. 1 Cor. vii. 3. *uxori vir debitam benevolentiam reddito: similiter autem et uxor viro.*

{¶6} Propria sunt vel mariti vel uxoris. Mariti, Exod. xxi. 10, 11. *alimentum ejus, operimentum ejus, et tempus ejus ne diminuito: quod si tria ista non fecerit ei—.* Prov. v. 18, 19. *lætare cum uxore adolescentiæ tuæ—.*

## en-sumner

<!-- p.680 -->
{¶1} Thus far we have treated of the virtues or special duties which man owes to his neighbour simply as such; we are next to consider those which originate in circumstances of particular relationship. These duties are either private or public.

{¶2} The private duties are partly domestic, and partly such as are exercised towards those not of our own house. Gen. xviii. 19. *I know him, that he will command his children, and his household after him, and they shall keep the way of Jehovah.* 1 Tim. v. 8. *if any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith, and is worse than an infidel.*

{¶3} Under domestic duties are comprehended the reciprocal obligations of husband and wife, parent and child, brethren and kinsmen, master and servant.

{¶4} **THE DUTIES OF HUSBAND AND WIFE** are mutual or personal.

{¶5} Mutual duties. 1 Cor. vii. 3. *let the husband render unto the wife due benevolence, and likewise also the wife unto the husband.*

{¶6} The personal duties appertaining to either party respectively are, first, those of the husband. Exod. xxi. 10, 11. *her food, her raiment, and her duty of marriage shall he not diminish; and if he do not these three unto*
<!-- p.681 -->
*her, &c.* Prov. v. 18, 19. *rejoice with the wife of thy youth.* Esther i. 22. *every man should bear rule in his own house.* 1 Cor. xi. 3. *I would have you know that the head of every man is Christ, and the head of the woman is the man.* Eph. v. 25. *husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church.* Col. iii. 19. *husbands, love your wives, and be not bitter against them.* 1 Pet. iii. 7. *likewise, ye husbands, dwell with them according to knowledge, giving honour unto the wife, as unto the weaker vessel.* The contrary is reproved Mal. ii. 13, 14, &c. *Jehovah hath been witness between thee and the wife of thy youth, against whom thou hast dealt treacherously—.* Prov. v. 20, 21. *why wilt thou, my son, be ravished with a strange woman?*

{¶7} Personal duties of the wife. Prov. xiv. 1. *every wise woman buildeth her house.* xix. 14. *a prudent wife is from Jehovah.* xxxi. 11, &c. *the heart of her husband doth safely trust in her.* 1 Cor. xi. 3, &c. *the woman is the glory of the man; for the man is not of the woman, but the woman of the man.* Eph. v. 22—24. *wives, submit yourselves unto your own husbands, as unto the Lord; for the husband is the head of the wife, even as Christ is the head of the church, and he is the Saviour of the body; therefore as the church is subject unto Christ, so let the wives be to their own husbands in every thing.* Col. iii. 18. *wives, submit yourselves unto your own husbands, as it is fit in the Lord.* Tit. ii. 4, 5. *that they may teach the young women to be sober, to love their husbands, to love their children, to be discreet, chaste, keepers at home, good, obedient to their own husbands, that the word of God be not blasphemed.* 1 Pet. iii. 1, &c. *likewise, ye wives, be in subjection to your own husbands,* &c. The same is implied in the original formation of the woman: Gen. ii. 22. *the rib which Jehovah had taken from man, made he a woman;* it cannot therefore be fitting that a single member, and that not one of the most important, should be independent of the whole body, and even of the head. Finally, such is the express declaration of God: Gen. iii. 16. *he shall rule over thee.*[^s7]

Exod. iv. 25. *a bloody husband art thou to me.* Job ii. 9. *then said his wife unto him, Dost thou still retain thine integrity?* &c. 2 Sam. vi. 20. *Michal the daughter of Saul came out to meet David, and said,* &c. Prov. ix. 13. *a foolish woman is clamorous.* vii. 11. *her feet abide not in her house.*[^s8] xiv. 1. *the foolish plucketh it down with her hands.* xix. 13. *the contentions of a wife are a continual dropping.* See also xxvii. 15. xxi. 9. *it is better to dwell in a corner of the house top, than with a brawling woman in a wide house.* v. 19. *it is better to dwell in the wilderness, than with a contentious and an angry woman.* See also xxv. 24. Eccles. vii. 26. *I find more bitter than death the woman whose heart is snares and nets, and her hands as bands: whoso pleaseth God shall escape from her, but the sinner shall be taken by her.* Above all, adultery: Deut. xxii. 14, 20. *I took this woman, and when I came unto her, I found her not a maid......if this thing be true,* &c.

{¶9} **THE DUTIES OF PARENTS** are inculcated Deut. iv. 9. *teach them thy sons, and thy sons' sons.* vi. 6, 7. *these words which I command thee this*

## headnote

**Place.** Having treated the duties owed a neighbour simply as a neighbour, Milton turns to those arising from particular relationships. This chapter takes the private and domestic side; II.xvi takes duties towards those outside the house, II.xvii the public ones.

**Argument.** The chapter is a scaffold of reciprocal pairs — husband and wife, parent and child, guardian and ward, teacher and pupil, elder and younger, superior and inferior, brethren and kinsmen, master and servant — each given its duties and then its opposites. Milton's own prose is skeletal, often no more than the naming of the pair; the substance is an almost unbroken chain of proof-texts. It closes with property in servants, their correction, and the manumission of Hebrew slaves.

**Pressure point.** The doctrine is conventional throughout — the wife subject, the rod commended, servitude regulated rather than questioned. What is contentious here is Sumner's, not Milton's: at a bare Proverbs proof-text the editor imports the *Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce* on what the Christian emperors held a divorcive fornication, and buttresses Gen. iii. 16 with *Paradise Lost* and *Tetrachordon*. A third note defends Milton against the jeer that he had been a schoolmaster.

rendered four different ways: "Offences against these duties" (¶8), "The opposites are, first,"

(¶38). Likewise `idem` becomes "See also" throughout (¶¶8, 13, 37). The `first / Secondly / Lastly`
enumeration habit is present but mild here (¶¶6, 10, 11).

**Small caps are handled DIFFERENTLY by the two layers.** The Latin small-caps the lemma word as the
paragraph's opening (`**TUTORUM** officia.`, `**PUPILLORUM.**`, `**SENIORUM.**`); the English embeds
it inside an English frame ("For the duties of **GUARDIANS**, see…", "The duties of **WARDS**."). Both
volumes agree that the word is emphasised; they disagree about where the emphasis sits in the
sentence. Transcribed as printed in each layer per CONVENTIONS §2 — this is not a case for
harmonising.

**Printed anomalies and accidentals — kept as printed (CONVENTIONS §6).**

- **La 515: `1 Sam. xix 20.`** — no point after `xix`, confirmed at 600 dpi. The English prints
  `1 Sam. xix. 20.` with the point, so this is a Latin-layer accidental, not a shared reading. Third
  of its kind in the corpus after the pilot's `1 Cor. viii 7.` and `1 Tim. i. 13`.
- **En: the volume is inconsistent with itself on one abbreviation within four pages** — `Col. iii. 19.`
  at En 681 but `Coloss. iii. 21.` at En 683, `Coloss. iii. 20.` at En 684, then `Col. iv. 1.` at
  En 688 and `Col. iii. 22` at En 689. Per CONVENTIONS §8b, follow each instance; do not normalise.
- **Ellipsis dot-counts vary and are transcribed as counted** — six dots is the norm (`......`), but
  En 687 sets four in `between thee and me....for we be brethren`, and En 688–689 set five
  (`plowing.....`, `despise them.....`). Per CONVENTIONS §2 the dots are counted per instance.
- **The Tetrachordon quotation in note 7 carries a long rule** (`————`) marking omitted text, distinct
  from the dotted ellipses used in the running text. Transcribed as a dash run.
- La 512 prints the proof-texts of Prov. out of numerical order (`ix. 13 … vii. 11 … xiv. 1 …`);
  Milton's own sequence, kept.
- La 518's lemma headings take **no point** before the citation (`De possessione et jure in servos
  Gen. xvii. 12.`, `De castigatione servorum Gen. xvi. 6.`). Kept.

**★ Milton cites the same three verses twice on one page, once for each half.** La 517 gives
`Prov. iii. 33`, `xiv. 11` and `xv. 6` in `HERI officia` for their blessing-clauses (*habitaculo
justorum benedicitur*; *tentorium rectorum florebit*; *in domo justi robur est magnum*) and then all
three again in the immediately following `Opposita` for their curse-clauses (*execratio Jehovæ
incumbit domui improbi*; *domus improborum perdetur*; *proventus improbi conturbatur ex sese*). The
English mirrors it exactly. **A scripture index that de-duplicates by reference would collapse six
uses into three and destroy precisely the antithesis Milton built** — flagged for M4, and the third
distinct way this corpus has now shown that the index cannot be built mechanically from either
layer (after II.x's omission and II.ix's reordering).
